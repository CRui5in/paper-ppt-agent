# Visual Composition and Finish

Apply these rules while planning and authoring slides. Preserve an assigned template's fixed brand elements and geometry; apply this guidance to the composition and new elements inside its editable regions.

## Ornament with a designed composition

Ornament is optional. A cover can be complete through typography, a composed color field, and balanced negative space. When ornament improves the page, develop one coherent visual motif with a clear anchor, scale, rhythm, and relationship to the text.

In the design specification, describe the motif's placement and extent, shape language, palette, stroke weights, spacing, and how it recurs on structural pages. The executor should develop that visual idea rather than assemble a fresh collection of unrelated shapes on each slide.

Simple geometry can look finished: a flowing contour band with continuous curvature and controlled spacing, a balanced composition of filled planes, or a carefully proportioned geometric field. Choose the form for this deck; these are examples, not a default cover template. Refinement comes from proportion, alignment, edge treatment, layering, and restraint, not a larger number of SVG elements.

Do not fill unused areas with a collection of generic sine waves, floating dot grids, concentric rings, disconnected strokes, or arbitrary intersecting paths. In particular, a wave strip plus a dot matrix plus a ring cluster in separate parts of an otherwise empty cover is unfinished decoration. Lower opacity alone does not resolve that fragmented composition.

For decorative curves, make curvature and tangent transitions smooth, spacing intentional, and line endings or edge crops deliberate. Keep overlaps legible and stroke weights consistent within the motif. Integrate the motif into one visual region, with contrast and scale subordinate to the title. A motif may contain repeated lines or points when they form a controlled composition. Preserve real chart marks, map contours, graph nodes, connectors, and scientific geometry according to their explanatory role.

## Content and layout

Establish the main reading point, then arrange the evidence and explanation in a clear sequence. Let the material determine whether the page needs a figure, comparison, process, equation, table, or typographic argument. Choose region sizes from the actual copy and visible figure bounds.

Keep the substance that makes the page useful: what is being explained or compared, the supporting result or mechanism, and the conditions needed to interpret it. Use specific headings and direct explanations. Do not replace evidence with generic slogans or reduce every region to a short label to make room for decoration. Speaker notes carry elaboration; the visible slide must still explain its main point.

Give the primary figure or explanation enough room to read at presentation size. Place annotations beside the feature they explain, establish shared edges and baselines, and distinguish section gaps from spacing inside a group. Use panels when they clarify actual groups; avoid turning unrelated material into identical cards. Break a long title at meaningful phrase boundaries and keep metadata at a quieter scale.

## Finish before delivery

Check the slide at thumbnail size for hierarchy and at presentation size for typography, figure detail, and geometry. Inspect the decorative layer as a whole: remove fragments that compete with the main motif, repair awkward tangencies or endpoints, and retain quiet space where it supports the composition. Check that supporting copy explains the visual and that captions, labels, and source conditions remain readable.

When a decorative treatment remains unresolved, simplify it to a coherent form or omit it. Keep a plain divider, a simple line, or open whitespace when it does its job well.
